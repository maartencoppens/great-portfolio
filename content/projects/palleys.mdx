Palleys turns a photo of your pet into a small, full-colour 3D-printed figure that comes as a keychain, a clip or a figurine. It started as our student company in UNIZO's STEP UP programme: six students in Ghent, one webshop, and half of the profit going to Dierenasiel Gent.

Customers upload a few photos of their cat, dog, rabbit or chameleon. An AI pipeline turns those photos into a print-ready 3D model, we check every step by hand, and a print partner produces it in full-colour resin before we finish and ship it from Ghent. Next to the custom Palleys, the shop also sells ready-made collectibles.

## My Role

I was the main developer and wrote nearly all of the code. Bavo and Reinhart designed the website in Figma, and I turned that design into a working shop, from the storefront to the backoffice and the server it runs on. My part:

- **Storefront.** The Next.js shop in Dutch and English, with the configurator, cart, checkout, returns portal and legal pages.
- **AI pipeline.** Joran came up with almost the entire pipeline and tested it by hand until it worked. I turned his process into software: the generation steps, the review screens and the status flow around them.
- **Backoffice.** Everything the team needs to run the company: reviewing models, orders, print batches, shipping labels, returns, discounts, finance and site settings.
- **Infrastructure.** Hosting on our own VPS with Docker and Caddy, plus storage, email and payments.

Outside the code, I helped a little with the logo, with the idea of a paw in the P. The rest of the team was just as essential. Joran also handled the print partner and post-production. Lies did the bookkeeping, Emie and Reinhart designed the flyers, cards and stickers, and Bavo ran our social media. Everyone helped test the shop and hunt down bugs.

## How It Works

The pipeline is Joran's: he tested which tools and prompts could turn a pet photo into something printable, long before there was any code. In the shop, a Palley goes through four stages before it is printed.

1. **Photos.** The customer uploads up to four photos. They go straight to private cloud storage through signed URLs, because a photo of someone's pet in their living room is personal data.
2. **Pose.** After payment, Nano Banana Pro (Gemini) turns the photos into one image of the pet lying down: a chunky figurine with no thin ears or tails that could break. Every species has its own prompt, because a snake and a hedgehog need very different instructions.
3. **3D model.** Meshy turns that pose into a textured 3D model.
4. **Print file.** In Blender, we scale the model to 37 mm, clean the mesh and bake the colours, then export it as a print file.

Every preview follows a fixed state machine, so a model can never skip a step or end up in a state the backoffice doesn't understand.

## Humans in the Loop, on Purpose

The most important design decision wasn't technical. Our time as a student team costs us nothing, but every AI generation costs money. So instead of a fully automatic pipeline, we put a person at every step that spends credits or reaches a customer. Nothing is generated before an order is paid. An admin approves the pose before Meshy starts, approves the model before it goes to Blender, and checks every item off before an order can ship. Generating a new pose is limited to three attempts, and an admin can always upload a pose or model made by hand instead.

Everything else is automated, so that the manual work is spent on judgement instead of admin. File names and order numbers are generated, so nobody types them by hand. Print batches are bundled into a single zip for the print partner, and shipping labels are exported as a CSV for bpost. The sales journal is ready to paste into our bookkeeping, and a scheduled job closes unpaid orders and deletes photos once they're no longer needed.

## The Challenge

The architecture changed twice while we were building it.

We started with Shopify as a headless backend, used only for the cart, checkout and orders. After connecting it, I realised we would only use a small part of what we were paying for. A cart, a checkout and an orders table were things I could build myself, and the monthly cost was hard to justify for a student company. So I replaced Shopify with our own cart, checkout and order system.

Payments took three attempts. Mollie needed a VAT number, which a STEP UP student company doesn't have. Bancontact Pro through our bank worked, but its payment page didn't feel trustworthy on the web: it's built for scanning a QR code at a physical counter, not for a webshop. In the end we moved to Stripe Checkout with Bancontact and iDEAL | Wero, which was easier for customers and for us. Because I had kept payments behind one small interface, each switch meant replacing a single provider instead of rewriting the checkout.

## Building with AI

We had a few weeks to go from idea to a working shop next to our other courses, so I built much of Palleys with AI coding tools. That only worked because I decided the architecture first. I adapted [Mees Akveld's React structure](https://www.meesakveld.be/en/insights/case-studies/finding-my-ideal-react-structure) for Next.js, with thin routes, one service per domain and a single entry point for the database. Every rule was written down, so code generated by AI had a clear place to go. Before bigger changes, like replacing Shopify, I wrote a plan in phases first and had the AI work through it one phase at a time.

AI made me faster, but it didn't decide what to build. I reviewed the changes, added over 400 automated tests for the parts where mistakes cost money, like payments, discounts and status changes, and threw away a lot of generated code that worked but didn't fit.

## Result

In about seven weeks, Palleys went from the first commit to a live shop at [palleys.be](https://palleys.be), running on our own server. The team runs the whole company from the backoffice, from approving a model to sending a batch to the printer to doing the bookkeeping.

## Looking Back

This is the project I'm proudest of, because it isn't a prototype: it's a real shop that takes real payments, and a whole team depends on it to run the company. It taught me that the hardest decisions in a product aren't about code, but about what to build yourself, what to pay for, and where a person should stay in control.

In a next version, I'd automate the Blender step and add a check that rejects photos without a pet before anything is generated.
